Early warning with the intelligence of slime mold.

A slime mold has no brain, yet it builds networks that engineers struggle to match. BioSense borrows that strategy from nature and turns it into seismic sensing and alert delivery that keeps improving, even when parts of the system are damaged.

Meet the slime mold

Nobody knows nature better than nature itself.

This is biomimicry: instead of inventing a solution from scratch, we study one that evolution has already tested. Physarum polycephalum, a slime mold, is a single giant cell with thousands of nuclei and not one neuron. It still finds the shortest path through a maze, and in a 2010 study in Science it grew a network with efficiency and fault tolerance comparable to the Tokyo rail system. Its method is a feedback loop: the more protoplasm flows through a tube, the wider that tube grows, while neglected tubes wither away.

We did not design the algorithm. We learned it from the slime mold.

Explore

A fan of fine tubes spreads in every direction, sampling many routes in parallel with no central planner.

Reinforce

Flow widens the tubes that carry it, a positive feedback loop. Tubes with little flow shrink and disappear.

Adapt

Sever a tube and the flow redistributes, so the network regrows a path around the wound.

From slime mold to algorithm.

BioSense maps each behavior of Physarum onto a part of the warning system, so the sensor network optimizes itself the way slime mold does.

Four steps, a few seconds.

Every extra second of warning lets people drop, cover and move away from danger.

Step 1

Detect

Slime mold optimized sensors catch the fastest signal first. For earthquakes, that is the P-wave.

Step 2

Recognize

Biomimetic pattern recognition separates genuine events from background noise.

Step 3

Route

Data flows along self-optimizing pathways, and if links break, it finds a new way.

Step 4

Relay

The first person to receive the alert becomes the distributor and passes it on, so there is no single point of failure.

The earthquake time gap

P-waves arrive first and are the early signal. The slower S-waves cause the destruction. The gap runs from seconds to tens of seconds depending on distance, and BioSense uses it as warning time.
